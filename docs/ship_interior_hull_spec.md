# Ship Interior and Hull Spec

2026-11-15 ·

## Orientation: a skyscraper standing on its tail (read this first)

Picture the Rocinante's skyscraper: a tall, narrow tower whose floors are stacked one above another and joined by a ladder through a hatch in the middle of each floor. The ship is that tower. It stands on its tail, 140 m tall and 10 m across, with the bow at the top and the engines at the bottom. Every deck is a round floor you would look down onto, and going to the next deck means climbing up or down the ladder. It is not a navy ship, and no deck runs along the ship's length.

- **Upright, always.** Draw and describe the ship standing on its tail: bow at the top, the two engine pods at the bottom. Up is toward the bow and down is toward the engines. Down is where thrust pushes you, so it is also where "down" is for the crew.
- **A deck is one whole round floor.** Each deck is a circular disc that fills the entire cross-section of the hull: about 10 m across and only 3 m from floor to ceiling, so wide and short, like a hockey puck or a pie tin. One deck is one room, or a ring of rooms around a clear centre. A room is never a long space running the length of the ship.
- **Decks stack vertically.** Eleven decks make up the four stacked bays: A, B and C have three each and D has two, each deck 3.2 m above the next, like the storeys of a tower that is only 10 m wide. The one exception is the 34 m missile hall between B and C, which is a single tall open space (a vertical shaft with the tubes in rings around it), not a stack of decks. From a side cutaway you see a tall stack of flat slabs, one on top of another; from above you see one circle.
- **Two stair shafts, one on each side.** Two 1.4 m round shafts run the full height of the stacked bays, one on the port side and one on the starboard side. Each is built into the cell wall, half inside the room and half in the annulus, carries a ladder, and opens into every deck through a 1.2 m round side door. People move between decks by climbing a shaft, never along a corridor. The centre of every deck stays clear for the room's own business: the CIC plot, the mess table, the thermal tower, the reactor.
- **If the ship must be drawn lying on its side** (a side profile or a cutaway), the decks are still floors across the hull: thin vertical slices every 3.2 m, like slices of a sausage, never one long horizontal floor from bow to stern. Standing the ship upright removes the problem, so prefer it.

**Do not draw the wet-navy model.** None of these belongs in any picture of this ship: a long deck or corridor running the length of the hull; an upper deck and a lower deck with the ship lying flat; rooms laid end to end along a passage with a door at each end; a floor drawn as the long side of the hull; portholes along the flank; a flat horizontal deck seen from the side with people standing along it. If an image looks like the inside of a submarine or a destroyer, it is wrong. The right picture looks like the inside of a tall silo or a lighthouse with round floors.

**Wording for an image prompt.** "A 140 m tall, 10 m wide cylindrical spaceship standing upright on its tail, engines at the bottom and bow at the top. Inside, it is a stack of round, flat, circular floors like hockey pucks, each 10 m across and 3 m tall, joined by two vertical ladder shafts, one built into each side wall, with a round door into every floor, and an open clear centre on each floor. It is a skyscraper standing on its tail, not a naval vessel with long decks. All hull surfaces are matte black; colour appears only as small cue marks."

## Design premise

The ship is a 140 m, round-section hull built as a string of seven sealed pressure cells, and the heat sink is the space between those cells and the outer skin. Inside, every room is a round disc (a "puck") stacked along the ship's axis, like a skyscraper standing on its tail with the engines at the bottom (the Orientation section above says exactly how to picture it). Everything below follows from the rules we have settled.

- **Ohio proportions.** Long, slender, circular cross-section, no sail or conn tower, missile tubes in two dorsal rows.
- **Circular rooms.** In space the hull holds air in rather than keeping water out, so the load is internal pressure. Cylinders and domed ends carry that in tension with the least metal; flat-sided rectangular rooms need heavy bracing. Round also gives round hatches, ring frames and the curved CIC we already have.
- **Rooms stack along the axis.** Each pressure cell is a drum lying along the ship's length, and its decks are discs (pucks) about 10 m across and 3 m thick, stacked fore to aft. A deck is not a long floor running the length of the ship, as on a navy vessel.
- **Down is aft.** The engines are at the aft end and fire aft whenever the ship burns. Under thrust the floor of every room is its aft face and the ceiling is its forward face. The ship flips end for end to brake, but the engines still push the crew toward the aft face, so "down" means the same thing in acceleration and in braking. At coast the ship is weightless and "down" is a convention.
- **The gap is the sink.** The ring of space between each round cell and the outer skin is tank volume: liquid lithium aft, where the heat is made, and water, air and stores forward.
- **Black hulls.** Every ship is black. Colour appears only as small cue marks (see Finish and colour cues).
- *(added)* Crew spaces sit mid-ship; the reactor sits aft behind a thick shield.
- *(added)* Every cell seals from its neighbours with a round hatch, so one breach or fire costs one cell.

**Where this departs from the walkable ship already built** (`ship_inventory_and_art_plan.md`, section 20):

- **Size:** 100 m long, 10 m beam becomes 140 m, 14 m. That is a scale of about 1.4 with the same proportions.
- **Decks:** the old walkable ship stacked flat rectangular decks one above another with the engines behind. Here each room is a round disc and the discs stack fore to aft, about three to a bay. The dome and keel levels of the first draft are gone: a "dome" is just the forward head of a bay, and the "keel" is a line along the ventral side, outside the cells.
- **Tubes:** 24 in two rows of 12 stays. "12 live at 6 missiles = 72" becomes 10 live at 6 = 60, which matches the 60-missile magazine decision and the engine's 48 / 6 / 6 split. Two more tubes carry the probe sets; 12 stay salvage tubes.
- **Heat:** "dorsal panels and keel runs" becomes the lithium annulus plus eight fold-out radiator blades.
- **Rooms:** all twelve room images are rectangles and would need regenerating, or can stand in until then.

## Hull form and general dimensions

The hull is 140 m long with a 14 m circular cross-section, a length-to-beam ratio of 10 against the Ohio's 13. It is shorter than an Ohio because the heat sink wants volume more than length. Figures marked "target" are design values to tune against the game's meters, not measurements.

The hull is a smooth matte-black teardrop with no sail. The dorsal line carries a flat tube deck (24 round caps), three laser domes, a flush mast well, the docking collar and a probe recovery cradle. Flanks carry four radiator-blade channels, an upper and a lower on each side, each holding two blades, and the point-defence mounts. The bow carries the sensor array and the muzzle of the keel coilgun.

The designed complement of about 75 is a military crew in three watch sections of 25: one on watch, one asleep, one awake and off duty. Five or six people now fly a hull built for fifteen times that, because after the Quiet only a few niche mercenary types go to space at all. That explains the empty racks, the unused mess and the cold compartments.

**Finish and colour cues.** All hulls are black (decided 15 November): ours and every other ship's. In the fiction the hull carries a matte, low-albedo cold paint, because everything out here hunts by heat, light and radar and a black, cold hull is the quietest. On screen a black ship against a dark ground is hard to read, so colour is used only as a cue, in thin marks and never as a paint scheme. The cues are a design necessity, so they are listed here and kept few (all proposals except the black).

| Cue | Where | Colours | What it tells the player |
|---|---|---|---|

| Damage | torn plates and scars | none painted; scorching and bare metal only | where a ship has been hit |

The interior keeps its own palette (dark teal-grey and olive painted steel with warm brass); this section covers the exterior only.

## Structure: round cells and the heat-sink annulus

The pressure hull is six bays in a line plus the airlock, which is its own sealed cell: seven cells in all. Bays A, B, C and D are stacks of round discs; the missile bay T is one long hall. All sit inside a round outer skin, and the ring of space between the cells and the skin is tank volume.

**The drum.** A bay is a drum lying along the ship's axis: 10.0 m inside diameter and 12.0 m long, closed at each end by a shallow dished head 1.2 m deep. Inside the 14 m skin it leaves a ring 2.0 m thick all round, about 75 m² in section, for tanks, structure and cable ways. Bay pitch is 12 m along the ship.

**The puck.** Each room is a disc across the drum. Standard deck pitch is 3.2 m: 2.9 m clear height and 0.3 m of floor structure. A bay holds three pucks between its two heads (3 × 3.2 m + 2 × 1.2 m = 12.0 m), numbered from the bow: A1, A2, A3, then B1, B2, B3, and so on. The last puck of a bay sits in the aft head, where the wall curves in, so it is the narrower 7.6 m disc. The forward head is free space above the first puck, which is why the control room has a dome.

|---|---|---|---|
| A (12–24) | control room (CIC), the forward head is its dome | officer berths and captain's stateroom | array hall |
| B (25.5–37.5) | mess and galley | junction, with the airlock chamber off its dorsal wall | utility |

In bay D the reactor stands on the axis and the two decks are taller to hold it (the 1.6 m shield plug at the forward end is the shield door between C and D). In bay A the forward head is a 2.0 m dome so the control room keeps its ceiling, and the array hall gives up 0.8 m of height to pay for it.

**Where the dome and keel went.** The first draft had four levels in each capsule: a dome above and a keel below. Neither is a level now. The dorsal gear that lived in the dome (airlock, mast well, laser terminals, gantries) sits in a side chamber and in dorsal wall niches. The keel is a line along the ventral side, outside the cells: the coilgun rail, the 1.2 m crawl, the two water mains and the keel reserve run there.

**The missile bay (T).** T is the one cell that is not a stack of pucks. It is a single 34 m drum, like the Ohio's missile compartment, because the tubes (2.3 m across, 8.6 m long) stand radially across it in two dorsal rows. Under thrust "down" is its aft bulkhead, so the hold is a 31 m shaft: the crew climb it or ride the gantry car, and the racks carry brace straps. At coast it is a long hall, and the ventral ordnance gallery is its walking strip. Tube geometry (checked 16 November): each cap sits flush on the dorsal skin line, so the first 2.0 m of the 8.6 m tube runs through the ring and only 6.6 m is inside the 10 m cell. The tube base ends 1.6 m past the axis, which leaves 3.4 m to the ventral wall, enough for the 3 m ordnance gallery.

**Drawing note (for reference images).** In a long section (side view), draw each bay as a short round-section drum with its rooms as flat discs stacked bow to stern, each disc's floor on its aft side, and a ladder shaft running down each side wall along the ship's length (one port, one starboard); the centres of the discs are clear. In a cross-section (looking down the axis), draw one round room, a single disc 10 m across, with its centre clear, a round shaft built into the wall on the port side and another on the starboard side, and the 2 m ring of tanks round it. Never draw horizontal decks running the length of the ship, and never stack levels across the 14 m beam (no dome on top, no keel below). The missile bay is the exception: a long hall with two rows of tubes standing across it.

**What fills the annulus.**

- **Forward half (bays A, B, front of T):** the water tanks (about 330 m³ in six baffled tanks, see Water and fuel stores), air and stores, plus cable ways. Water is cheap radiation and micrometeoroid shielding, and it keeps the neutrino-line detector tank (Castellan's reactor channel) next to the sensor array. It must never touch lithium, which is why the two zones are separated by a bulkhead mid-way along the missile bay.
- **Aft half (rear of T, C, D, R):** liquid lithium, about 1,600 m³ in tank groups of 200 to 400 m³. See the heat sink section.
- **Bay T's dorsal band.** In bay T the 12 tube sleeves cross the ring, two rows 4.4 m apart, so about 60 degrees of the ring on the dorsal side is given to the sleeves and cable ways. Tank arcs there are flank arcs only, roughly a sixth less tank volume than in a bare bay.
- **Groups are arcs.** A tank group is a half-ring or a flank arc of the ring, and a damage zone of its own. The ring holds about 75 m³ per metre of ship, so a 250 m³ group fills about half of a half-ring round a 12 m bay; the rest is frames, insulation, pipework and crawl space.
- **Between tank and skin:** a 0.15 m vacuum gap and multilayer insulation, so the outer skin stays near background temperature while the lithium behind it is at 450 to 950 K. A damaged seam shows as a hot stripe on the hull.

**Orientation.** The engines are aft, so under thrust "down" points aft and the floor of each room is its aft face. That is the physical basis for couches tilted and gimballed for burns and for the brace state. At coast the ship is weightless and decks are a naming convention. Decision (15 November): gravity is Newtonian only. There is no gravity generator, and spun sections are out because spinning machinery adds heat. Floors are magnetised plates on each room's aft face that hold grip soles and tools, with rails everywhere. Because nothing needs a fixed "down", rooms are round discs and drums (decided 15 November; the first draft had kept vertical capsules).

**Sealing.** Bays join through 1.5 m long round transit tunnels on the axis, with a 1.2 m hatch at each end. The reactor bay's forward tunnel doubles as a shield door: 0.4 m of lithium-hydride and boron carbide, with the hatch set into it. The two stair shafts pass from bay to bay through their own 1.5 m tunnels at the wall, with a 1.2 m door at each end.

## Weapons: missile battery, coilgun and point defence

The ship carries 24 tubes in two dorsal rows, standing radially across the missile hold, a fixed keel coilgun and kinetic point-defence turrets. Point defence is kinetic flak, not lasers; the reasons are at the end of this section.

**The missile.** 0.46 m across, 4.6 m long, about 550 kg (target). Six sit on a 1.24 m pitch circle around a 0.6 m handling hub, forming a revolver drum 1.95 m across and about 4.5 t loaded.

**The tube.** 2.3 m outside diameter, 8.6 m long, standing radially across the hold (dorsal to ventral): 4.6 m of missile, 1.2 m of hub and indexing motor, 1.2 m of ejector plenum, 1.6 m of seal, gas generator and cap. The cap sits flush in the tube deck on the dorsal line, and the tube reaches inward to a base housing near the hold's axis, spanning the hold as an Ohio tube spans three decks. Of the 8.6 m, 2.0 m lies in the ring and 6.6 m in the cell, so the base stops 1.6 m past the axis, with 3.4 m clear to the ventral wall (the ordnance gallery needs 3.0 m). Tubes are 2.45 m apart along a row (29.4 m for twelve) and the rows are 4.4 m apart, which leaves a 2.1 m lane between them under an overhead gantry.

**Assignment** (tubes numbered 1 to 12 from the bow in each row):

| Tubes | Count | Load |
|---|---|---|
| Starboard 1, port 1 | 2 | probe sets A and B (a set of 3, 4 or 6 fits a six-pocket drum) |
| Starboard 2–6, port 2–6 | 10 | missiles: eight HE tubes (48), one EMP (6), one flare (6) = 60, one salvo per tube |
| Starboard 7–12, port 7–12 | 12 | salvage canisters (cargo) |

Reloading is a dock job. The yard strikes a drum down through the cap with a crane, which cannot happen underway. The twelve salvage tubes are the ship's strongroom: heavily shielded and sealed, they are where smuggled AI-touched salvage rides. Decision (15 November): that stored salvage does not count toward the integration meter and does not appear in the Tally.

**Sleeping beside the battery.** Each tube ends in a ring-shaped base housing (indexer, hub drive) about 3 m across. Crew racks fill the 1.4 m strips down both flanks and the wedges between base housings: 24 racks in three pods of eight, stacked three high. The forward pod sleeps beside the live tubes, and each launch is a thump through the bunk frames from the ejector gas. The two made-up visitor racks are in the aft pod beside the salvage tubes. Warheads stay inert until armed in flight.

**Coilgun.** A fixed rail along the keel line from a breech in engineering lower (D2) to a muzzle in the bow, about 92 m long with a 0.12 m bore. A 2 GJ pulse-capacitor bank (no flywheels, since spinning adds heat) gives one 20 kg slug at about 15 km/s; the reactor recharges it in about 20 s. It fires only dead ahead, so using it means pointing the ship.

**Point defence.** Six twin-barrel turrets of about 30 mm, in armoured blisters that retract flush: a dorsal and a ventral pair of mounts forward (x about 19), a port and starboard pair amidships (x about 56) and a dorsal and ventral pair aft (x about 108). Rounds are proximity-fused fragmentation shells that burst into a pellet cloud on the computed track; muzzle velocity about 2 km/s, useful range 1.5 to 8 km (all targets). Each mount holds about 3,000 rounds in a drum fed from the nearest keel store. Four flush phased-array radar and lidar heads on the flanks track targets. Coverage is nearly spherical; the weak sector is straight astern, where the engine plumes blind the trackers.

The three game states map onto hardware:

- **Off:** turrets stowed, trackers dark.
- **Armed:** blisters open, turrets slewed by the passive array and their own cold optical and infrared stare. Almost silent, slow to react.
- **Active:** radar and lidar lit, turrets tracking, spun up within seconds. Bright emission that anything looking can see.

**Why kinetic and not laser.** This matches the existing design ("automated cannons throw a wall of shrapnel", with the lit trackers as the cost). Three further reasons fit the game. A laser puts every joule it uses into the heat sink, and a kill costs far more heat than a burst of shells. A laser's emission is brighter and longer than a tracker's. And kinetic fire has a finite magazine, which gives a long fight a cost. A laser turret stays available as an optional war-era part, a Vask line-fleet refit with its own lithium loop, heavy and thirsty. The flak cloud also scuffs the hull when the ship flies through it, which suits the near-miss damage already in canon.

## Probes, laser signalling and sensors

Probes launch from the two forward tubes, signal by laser through three dorsal and ventral terminals, and the ship reads the world through a bow array plus a neutrino-line tank.

**Probe ports.** Starboard 1 and port 1 each hold one probe set. A probe is about 0.30 m by 2.0 m and 60 kg (target), carried in an adapter sleeve inside the same revolver drum the missiles use. Sets are matched arrays (shared calibration, clocks and link keys), so probes from different sets never mix. A set of 3, 4 or 6 fills the six pockets; spare sets sit in the ventral ordnance gallery and are swapped at dock. Probes are gas-ejected and cannot be reloaded in battle.

**Recovery.** A cradle on the dorsal fairing just aft of the docking collar (x about 33) carries a 4 m folding arm. Recovery starts with a radio hail, so it is loud and normally happens after the fight, competing with salvage time.

**Laser terminals.** Three steerable 1.0 m domes with 0.6 m apertures and about 5 µrad pointing: L1 dorsal forward (x about 17), L2 dorsal aft (x about 84), L3 ventral aft (x about 100). L1 and L2 cover the upper hemisphere; L3 covers below, and the hull blocks the rest. The beam is narrow and aimed at where the ship will be, so a link survives only while heading holds within about 3 degrees and speed within about 5 percent (the design's link tolerance). Wire-guided missiles follow the same rule: turning cuts the wire.

**Radio.** A 3 m mast rises from a flush well at x about 30, with patch antennas on the flanks. It is omnidirectional and loud. There is no sail: the mast is a rod, stowed in the fairing.

**Sensors.** A 5.5 m all-round passive array fills the bow fairing, with conformal strips along the flanks. It feeds the three channels the game uses: heat, reactor line and eclipse (the visual silhouette against a bright backdrop). The reactor line comes from a gadolinium-doped water tank of about 120 m³ in the forward annulus, behind the array hall. Active ping transmitters are in the bow and are the brightest emission the ship makes. A trailed-line winch (aft pylon notch) and a directional dish (dorsal mount) are refit options, as the tech-choices note describes: a trailed line is lost on any hard turn, and a dish only sees where it points.

## Power, main engines and maneuvering thrusters

An Ohio-sized fission reactor runs the ship; two separate fusion main engines move it. That split is forced by arithmetic: pushing 11,000 t at even 0.05 g takes about 5.4 MN, and at a 300 km/s exhaust that is roughly 800 GW of jet power. No submarine reactor comes near it, so the mains carry their own cores. Accepted 15 November.

**Reactor.** One lithium-cooled fission plant of about 450 MWt (target).

- **Vessel:** 3.2 m across and 8.0 m long, with a core of 1.4 m by 1.5 m. It stands on the ship's axis in bay D: base on the aft deck (engineering lower), casing rising forward through the grating of engineering upper, so under thrust it stands on its base like a column.
- **Coolant:** isotopically pure lithium-7, to avoid breeding tritium. Electromagnetic pumps have no moving parts, so they are silent, but they draw about 3 MW. Electricity comes from a liquid-metal magnetohydrodynamic generator and thermoelectric stages in the loop, with no turbines, because spinning machinery adds heat and noise.
- **Shield:** an 8.4 m well, 1.6 m thick, of lithium hydride, boron carbide and a lithium jacket. Reactor plant with shield: about 1,600 t.
- **Output:** about 30 MWt at idle (hotel load), up to 450 MWt for coilgun recharge, active trackers and pings, and spooling the mains.
- **Decay heat:** after a scram the core still gives about 30 MW, falling to 1 percent over an hour. The sink absorbs it. With a full sink and the radiator blades damaged, the core can overheat, which is the hull-melt risk already in the engine.
- **Crew:** engineering upper is cleared at redline.

**Main engines.** Two fusion pods, each 18 m long and 3.2 m across, about 900 t (target), on pylons 4.6 m either side of the centreline and tucked close to the aft taper, which is what "tight" means here. Thrust lines pass through the centre of mass, and each pod gimbals about 5 degrees for trim.

- **Coupling:** a 2.4 m ring on each pylon carries power, magnet control and two 0.4 m lithium lines. This is the coupling Castellan inspects; a drive swap at a yard is a coupling job.
- **Bell:** open or ducted, per the tech-choices note. A ducted bell has its knee at a higher speed rung.
- **Thrust (target):** 100 percent is about 0.15 g, redline about 0.4 g, an emergency burn about 2 to 3 g for seconds, limited by reaction mass (below). Braced couches are advised above about 1 g.
- **Spool:** the pods need start energy from the reactor and a spool time (about 3 game minutes at standard in the engine).
- **Heat to the hull:** the pods' waste heat goes into the lithium sink. Blanket, shield and coil losses ride the two 0.4 m lithium lines through the pylon couplings back to the sink loop: about 270 MW at 100 percent drive (scaling roughly with drive power), plus 25 to 30 MW from the plant, so about 300 MW in all. At 270 MW the lines carry about 130 kg/s of lithium on a 500 K rise, roughly 0.26 m³/s or about 1 m/s in each line, so the plumbing is credible. The vent fins reject that heat to space while they are out (see Vent fins); with the fins retracted for silent running it all stays in the sink and the sink warms. Scale warning: the cited design loses about 25 percent of its fusion power as heat. A 270 MW heat budget is a quarter of about 1.1 GW of fusion power, which that design would turn into a drive of about 10 kN, not 16 MN. This ship's 0.15 g needs about 640 GW of jet power, so it is a torch drive about 1,500 times beyond the cited design, and its waste heat is only about 0.04 percent of its jet power. That is a deliberate piece of fiction and the one big leap in the engine spec; the sink model depends on it, and everything else here follows real concepts.

**Maneuvering thrusters.** Eight clusters of four nozzles, 32 in all: four clusters forward (x about 10) and four aft (x about 112), spaced round the hull. Each nozzle gives about 25 kN (target), and together they handle pitch, yaw, roll and translation. They are the "anchor" creep drive: about 0.001 g for quiet trim, with low heat, and without the pods. They burn steam from the water tanks, heated through the lithium loop, and are for attitude, docking and settling the water before a burn rather than for travel: an hour of translation at 0.001 g would cost about 190 t, so creeping is quiet but far from free.

**Reaction mass: water.** The pods are fusion torches in the style of the Direct Fusion Drive being developed at Princeton Plasma Physics Laboratory and Princeton Satellite Systems ([paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2002.12686)). It burns deuterium and helium-3 in a field-reversed plasma and passes cold propellant around it, so the fusion products heat the propellant before a magnetic nozzle expels it. Adding propellant lowers the exhaust velocity and raises the thrust, which is the throttle for every mode in the table below; adding propellant to the exhaust at the cost of exhaust velocity is also how other fusion concepts get thrust ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fusion_rocket)). This ship's yard pod does the same with water (decided 15 November): the stream is split and ionised into hydrogen and oxygen and thrown at about 80 km/s. That is hot (oxygen ions at roughly 500 eV) but within what the lost-technology pod can do, and because jet power depends only on flow and exhaust velocity, the heavier ions change none of the numbers below. Water is the best bulk reaction mass for the ship: it is dense (1 t/m³ against 0.17 for liquid deuterium, so 250 t is 250 m³ and not 1,500), liquid at room temperature, common beyond the frost line, and it is also the crew's drinking water, air and shielding.

**Water tanks (target):** about 330 m³ in six baffled tanks in the forward annulus around bays A and B and the front of T: four drive tanks of 62.5 m³ (250 t), a 20 m³ crew reserve and a 60 m³ settling and filter tank. Two 0.4 m feed mains run aft along the keel line to the pod roots, and the same water feeds the maneuvering thrusters. Bay R and the aft annulus carry lithium, pumps and machinery only. See Water and fuel stores.

**Science versus fiction: the size of the drive.** The cited Direct Fusion Drive ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12686)) is a 1 to 2 MW machine: 8 to 11 N of thrust, specific impulse of 10,000 to 12,500 s, about 1 kW/kg, a propellant feed of 0.08 to 0.7 g/s, and 25 percent of its fusion power lost as heat. The ship's drive is the same idea made enormously larger:

| Quantity | Cited design | This ship (100 percent) | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fusion power | 1 to 2 MW (simulated 0.25 to 7 MW) | about 1.3 TW | about 10⁶ |
| Thrust | 8 to 11 N | 16 MN | about 1.5 million |
| Specific power | about 1 kW/kg | about 720 kW/kg (1.3 TW over 1,800 t of pods) | about 700 |
| Thrust per tonne of drive | about 0.008 N/kg | about 9 N/kg | about 1,100 |
| Specific impulse | 10,000 to 12,500 s | 8,000 s (cruise 15,000 s) | about 1 |
| Propellant flow | 0.08 to 0.7 g/s | 200 kg/s | 3 × 10⁵ to 2.5 × 10⁶ |
| Waste heat | 25 percent of fusion power | 270 MW, 0.02 percent | about 1,200 times cleaner |

So the fiction is not a little oversize. Per tonne the pods are about 1,000 times better than the best published concept, and the heat they leave in the hull is about 1,000 times smaller. The specific impulse is the one number that stays in the real range.

Why it cannot be much smaller at hunt tempo. The hunt's speed rungs are 80 m/s at 80 percent and 150 m/s at redline, and the crew expect to reach them in a minute or two. Scaling the same pods down in capability gives:

| Oversize against the cited design, per tonne | 100 percent thrust | Time to 80 m/s |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (the paper) | 0.00013 g | about 17 hours |
| 10 | 0.0013 g | about 1.7 hours |
| 100 | 0.013 g | about 10 minutes |
| 1,000 (this spec) | 0.13 to 0.15 g | about 1 minute |

So 0.15 g is the tempo of the game written as an engine, and the thrust figures in this spec are descriptive: the speed rungs, not the g figure, are what the engine plays. The heat is a separate leap: even at 100 times oversize, the paper's 25 percent would leave about 45 GW in the hull, so the 270 MW budget needs pods about 1,000 times cleaner than the paper however large they are. The recommendation is to keep the drive as it is and say so plainly: an old, very advanced yard-built pod, the best machine this ship has, which nothing now being built can match.

What a 250 t tank buys in each mode (targets; flow is thrust divided by exhaust velocity):

| Mode | Specific impulse | Thrust | Flow | A full tank lasts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cruise (high Isp) | 15,000 s | 0.8 MN (5 percent, about 0.007 g) | 5.4 kg/s | about 13 hours |
| 100 percent | 8,000 s | 16 MN (0.15 g) | 200 kg/s | about 21 minutes |
| Redline | 3,000 s | 43 MN (0.4 g) | 1.5 t/s | about 3 minutes |
| Emergency burn | about 1,000 s | 270 MN (2.5 g) | 27 t/s | about 9 seconds |
| Creep thrusters (steam) | about 200 s | 0.1 MN (0.001 g) | 53 kg/s | about 80 minutes |

A full tank is about 1.8 km/s of delta-v at 100 percent settings and about 3.4 km/s in cruise mode (250 t out of about 11,000 t). Creep is very expensive: an hour at 0.001 g spends about 190 t, three quarters of a tank, to change speed by about 35 m/s. The pods in cruise mode make the same change for about 2.6 t, about seventy times less, but run hot and light a plume. So quiet is costly and efficient is loud, which is a real trade for the hunt.

## Water and fuel stores

The ship carries two things its pods consume, on very different clocks (decided 15 November). **Water** is the reaction mass the pods throw. It sets how far and how fast the ship can go, and it is bulk, cheap and common. **Fuel cassettes** are the sealed fusion fuel the pods burn. They are small, dense and valuable, and they set how long the pods can run and whether the ship can jump. The fission plant is a third clock that never matters on a sortie: about a gram of fissile fuel per megawatt-day, about 11 kg a year, years between yard refits.

**Water holding tanks.** Six baffled tanks, about 330 m³ (330 t when full), in the forward annulus around bays A and B and the front of T, behind a bulkhead from the lithium zone.

- **Four drive tanks** of 62.5 m³ (250 t in all). They feed the pods and the maneuvering thrusters through two 0.4 m mains that run aft along the keel line and cross-connect at the pod roots. Flow is under 1 m/s in each line at 100 percent drive and about 6 m/s at redline. The crew drain the tanks in a set order that alternates forward and middle tanks, so the ship does not turn tail-heavy as it empties.
- **A 20 m³ crew reserve** with its own feed, locked from the drive by a guarded BURN RESERVE valve. Drinking water, hygiene and the electrolysis that makes oxygen draw on it. Burning it all is worth about 140 m/s of delta-v and leaves the crew on recycled water for weeks, not for good. It is an emergency choice at the helm.
- **A 60 m³ settling and filter tank.** Melted ice and transferred water go here first, are filtered, and are then pumped to a drive tank (see Where water comes from).
- **Shielding.** Water between the crew and the outside is cheap radiation and splinter protection, which is why the tanks sit forward around the crew bays. A hit on a full tank loses water; see the risks below.
- **Settling (proposal).** In free fall water floats and the feed line can ingest steam bubbles. Surface-tension screens and bladders keep the feed ends wetted, and before a burn that follows a flip or a long coast the pods need about 30 seconds of creep thrust (0.001 g) to settle the water at the feed ends. The planner inserts that settling step before each burn, so a flip is also a short, quiet moment before the drive can start.
- **Freeze protection.** Tank and line heaters take about 0.2 MW (target). A long silent run with the heaters off lets the lines freeze in hours.
- **The gauge.** One gauge for the hunt, with the reserve line marked.

**Fuel cassettes.** A cassette is a sealed cryogenic cartridge of about 4.6 kg of deuterium and helium-3 mix (about 2.8 kg of helium-3 and 1.8 kg of deuterium), roughly 60 litres, a drum about 0.4 m across. One cassette is exactly one flat-out tank of burn (21 minutes of 100 percent drive).

- **Magazine.** Eight cassettes (about 37 kg) in a shielded vault on the reactor bay's feed. They are the most valuable cargo aboard, a place the crew can guard, steal from or hide things in.
- **Draw.** The pods draw fuel in proportion to pod power: one cassette per 21 minutes flat out, a few percent of that at cruise settings. A breach-drive spool draws one cassette (the start energy is the biggest single call on the plant; a game rule). The fission plant carries the hotel load and uses none.
- **Out of cassettes.** The pods are dead. The ship has the fission plant and the creep thrusters only: no breach drive, no real change of velocity.
- **Grades.** **Full** cassettes carry deuterium and helium-3 and give full performance. **Lean** cassettes are deuterium only: thrust is capped at about 30 percent, there are more neutrons and a little tritium, and the lithium blanket runs hotter. Lean cassettes are cheaper and commoner, which makes them the stop-gap that a salvage run can find and a reason to hoard full ones.
- **Control.** Helium-3 comes from lunar regolith or gas-giant skimming in the fiction (the Daedalus starship study's source). Helium-3 and heavy water in quantity are controlled goods: industrial heavy water is already under government and IAEA control because of its link to weapons, so a large purchase leaves a record and the interdict watches the depots.

**Where water comes from.**

- **Depot or tender (the default).** Warm liquid water is pumped aboard, about two hours for 250 t alongside. It is cheap, and the cost is exposure: a depot is a place where the ship can be found, and a purchase is a record.
- **Derelict transfer.** Wrecks and hulks carry their own tanks, which can be drained through the docking collar and a hose: 20 to 200 t a prize, hours of work, and the tanks may be damaged or fouled. No cryogenics are needed, so it is simple work, and a natural salvage job and a reason to board. Derelict ice haulers are the best prize.
- **Ice bodies.** Comets, asteroid ice, moons and ring particles. Crews with cutters and a collection net work at about 100 t an hour, and melting is the bottleneck: 250 t of ice at 100 K takes about 170 GJ, roughly two hours of the whole 30 MW plant. So a tank costs about three hours parked, with people outside and the melt plume and heaters glowing. A good salvage-style mission that can be interrupted.

**Purity grades (proposal).** **Clean** (grade A): full performance. **Hard** (grade B): salts and carbonates; filter cartridges are used up (a stores item) and the pods derate about 5 percent. **Foul** (grade C): ammonia, sulfates and dust; the pods derate about 10 percent and may trip, and a second filtration pass takes hours.

**Risks (a lot of the game).**

- **Fouled feed.** Dirty water clogs screens and filters and derates the pods.
- **Freezing.** A long silent run with the heaters off can plug a line.
- **Slosh and gas ingestion.** Starting a burn right after a flip, without the settling step, risks a steam bubble in the feed and a pod trip.
- **A hit on a water tank.** It leaks as a steam-and-ice plume, bright in sunlight, losing tens of tonnes a minute, and the ship's water is its range. Damage control plugs it.
- **Water meets lithium.** Never. The zones are separated by a bulkhead mid-way along the missile bay, and a lithium rupture beside a water tank would be a violent steam and hydrogen event.
- **Parked to mine.** Heaters and the melt plume are a signature, the ice may outgas or tumble, and a rival may want the same ice.
- **Bad salvage.** Mislabelled tanks, mixed grades, and tanks left by someone who did not plan to come back. Cassettes from a reactor loop carry tritium; a cassette's label is a thing to inspect.
- **Cassette crime.** Theft, forged labels and records: the most valuable thing aboard is also the most traceable.

## Heat sink

About 1,600 m³ of liquid lithium, roughly 800 t, stores about 1.7 TJ across a 500 K swing, and the game's 100 percent sink is the usable 1.4 TJ of that. Lithium suits the job: its specific heat is about 4.2 kJ/kg·K, the highest of any liquid metal, it stays liquid from 454 K to 1,615 K, and it shields neutrons. The sums, all targets:

- 1,600 m³ × about 500 kg/m³ × 4.2 kJ/kg·K × 500 K ≈ 1.7 TJ.
- With the fins retracted, full drive (about 300 MW to the sink) fills a cold sink in about 78 minutes; one flat-out propellant tank (about 21 minutes) uses a little over a quarter of it.
- At hotel load (25 to 30 MW) with the fins retracted, which is silent running, a cold sink lasts 13 to 16 hours. With the fins out the sink settles at a resting level of about 35 to 40 percent, so silent running normally starts from there and gives about 8 hours to the 95 percent line.

**Tank groups** (each is a damage-control zone):

| Group | Location | Volume |
|---|---|---|
| T-aft flank tanks, port and starboard | ring arcs either side of the rear half of the missile bay | 2 × 250 m³ |
| Thermal annulus, dorsal and ventral | half-rings round bay C | 2 × 250 m³ |
| Engineering annulus, two halves | half-rings round bay D | 2 × 200 m³ |
| Keel reserve | keel line, bays C to D | 200 m³ |

**Loops.**

1. The reactor's primary lithium loop passes through exchangers in the thermal tower (the room Dace runs).
2. A secondary loop fed by electromagnetic pumps carries heat to the tank groups, through valve manifolds in the sink machinery hall below.
3. Two more lines run to the engine pods through the pylon couplings.

The keel reserve is kept solid below 454 K behind extra insulation. Its latent heat of fusion (about 430 kJ/kg, roughly 43 GJ over 100 t) is a cold reserve that absorbs heat before it starts to warm. It is the natural home of the "Reservoir" part.

**Venting.** Eight radiator blades, 36 m by 6 m each, fold flush into four flank channels and present about 3,500 m² when out (see Vent fins). At the sink's 900 K they reject about 110 MW, and about 135 MW at 950 K. That carries the hotel load and about a third of full drive indefinitely; beyond that the sink fills, and with the fins retracted all of the load stays in it. Extended blades glow and are visible from a long way; this is the "cooling fins out: we are fully visible" state.

**Vent fins.** The fins are the ship's only way to dump heat to space, so they are modelled as hardware: where they stow, what they are made of, how big they are, and what they cost to use.

- **Where they come from.** Four flank channels run along the hull, an upper and a lower on each side, set about 40 degrees above and below the equator, from about x = 24 to x = 96. Each channel holds two blades end to end. The channels are recessed about 0.7 m into the outer lithium annulus with an insulated liner and a sprung cover, and sit between the dorsal tube doors and the flank point-defence mounts on the equator. Because the blades open radially, they lie edge-on to the thrust line.
- **What they look like.** Each blade is a flat three-leaf panel, 36 m long and 6 m deep when open, three leaves of 2 m hinged along the length like a fan. A leaf is a carbon-carbon sandwich with sodium or potassium heat pipes inside and a matte black high-emissivity coating, the same family as current space-reactor radiator studies. A lithium header along the hinge line feeds the heat pipes from a flank main tapped off the secondary loop at the thermal tower. Stowed, the leaves fold into a stack 2 m wide and about 0.5 m thick that lies flush in its channel.
- **How big.** 36 m by 6 m, radiating from both faces: about 430 m² per blade and about 3,460 m² for the eight. Mass is about 25 t with hinges and actuators (target).
- **How much they reject.** About 70 MW at 800 K, 110 MW at 900 K and 135 MW at 950 K, assuming the blades run at the sink's temperature. Output rises with the fourth power of temperature, so the sink finds its own resting level (table below). One blade is an eighth: about 17 MW at 950 K.
- **Deploying.** Out in about 60 seconds. In takes about 90 seconds, because the header drains back first and then the leaves fold, and the leaves stay hot for another minute or two, so going silent is not instant.
- **How they look to a sensor.** At 900 K the emission peaks near 3 µm, in the mid-infrared, so the fins are an infrared beacon at any setting. Above about 800 K (the Draper point) they also glow dull red to the eye, which is about 70 percent on the sink gauge.
- **Limits and damage.** The blades are rated to about 0.5 g edge-on, so an emergency burn above that strips any blade left out: the crew folds them first (90 seconds) or loses them. Flak splinters and hits tear leaves; one punctured heat pipe costs a sliver, a lost blade costs an eighth. Open blades also add radar cross-section.

**How the sink behaves.** Pod and plant heat go into the sink. With the fins out, the sink settles where rejection equals load, and with the fins retracted it only warms. The gauge is stored heat above the 450 K coolant, so 0 percent is cold and 100 percent is about 950 K (targets).

| Mode | Load to the sink | Fins | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silent running, pods cold | 25 to 30 MW | in | Warms from its resting level (about 38 percent) to the 95 percent line in about 8 hours; from cold, 13 to 16 hours |
| Coasting at hotel load | 25 to 30 MW | out | Settles at 34 to 40 percent and stays |
| Cruise, 10 percent drive | about 55 MW | out | Settles at about 62 percent |
| Patrol, 20 percent drive | about 80 MW | out | Settles at about 78 percent, fins faintly red |
| Sustained limit, about 35 percent drive | about 120 MW | out | Settles at the 95 percent fire line, no margin |
| Flat out, 100 percent drive | about 300 MW | out | 95 percent in about an hour; a propellant tank (21 minutes) ends first |
| Flat out, fins in | about 300 MW | in | 95 percent in about 45 minutes; a tank uses a little over a quarter of the sink |
| Fighting: plant at 450 MWt, 30 percent drive | about 530 MW | out | 95 percent in about 30 minutes; about 25 with the fins in |

So in an ordinary hunt the propellant tank, not the sink, is the first limit on drive. The sink is the limit when the fins are in, damaged, or the ship is fighting, and that is where the fires of the Hazards paragraph come from.

**Hazards.** Lithium burns in air and in nitrogen, and water makes it worse. A tank breach inside a cell starts a Class D fire that foam and water cannot put out. Venting the cell to vacuum does not put these fires out. A lithium-ion pack in thermal runaway carries its own oxidiser in the cathode and keeps burning without air, and hot lithium metal reacts with nitrogen, structure and spilled coolant, so vacuum removes the crew's air but not the fire. The ship's own lithium-ion banks (pulse buffering for trackers and pings, and the cold-start reserve for the reactor) fail the same way. The damage-control choices are to isolate and drain the tank group to the keel reserve, to smother metal fires with dry powder, to flood a runaway battery rack with its cooling loop, or to jettison the burning pack or tank section through the ventral hatch or a salvage tube. Venting the cell only buys time and costs its air. A breach on the outer skin spills lithium that freezes against the hull and shows as a bright hot patch for the first minutes. Damage-control calls should name the tank group, which gives "save the sink" and "save the drive" a physical meaning. Decision (15 November): lithium fires are the consequence of overloading the sink. Above about 95 percent a hot tank group starts to vent lithium vapour and any weak seam can fail, so the fire is the payoff of running hot, not random damage.

**Fire containment.** Every tank group is its own fire zone, and the response is a ladder in which each rung costs time. Decision (15 November): the ejection mechanism stays. A fire starts an automatic ejection countdown on the damage-control panel, and the captain can override it from the panel (below). Under the usual autonomy setting, Auto damage control climbs rungs 1 to 4 by itself.

1. **Alarm.** At about 90 percent on a group, or on a hot spot, a vapour reading or a battery-rack warning. The fire state begins at 95 percent with a weak seam, a breach, or a rack in runaway.
2. **Isolate** (about 20 seconds). Close the group's manifold valves, stop its pumps, seal the cell bulkheads and clear the cell. Its air stays in, because venting does not put these fires out.
3. **Drain** (about 5 minutes). Pump the lithium to the keel reserve, whose cold mass absorbs the heat. The reserve takes 200 m³ and then it is full, so it serves one drain, a 250 m³ group is not quite emptied, and it is no use at all if the keel group is the one burning.
4. **Smother** (about 2 minutes). Argon flood and Class D powder (graphite or copper based) on the metal. Never water, foam, carbon dioxide or nitrogen, which lithium reacts with.
5. **Cool.** A battery pack in runaway is flooded with its own cooling loop, because it needs heat taken away, not oxygen.
6. **Eject** (about 10 seconds). The last resort, and what happens by default: when the countdown reaches zero, bolts fire and gas thrusters push the whole group away from the hull. The captain can also eject at once from the panel.

The clock (targets): left alone, a group fire reaches the next group's frame in about 6 minutes and breaches the pressure cell behind it in about 10. A drain started at the alarm beats the spread clock; one started late does not, and then the choice is eject. The 30-second ejection countdown runs first, so attempting a drain means overriding it.

**Countdown and override (decision).** When a group goes to FIRE, the damage-control panel starts an auto-eject countdown for that group: a large red timer with the fire's intensity bar beside it.

- **Length.** 30 seconds from the fire state (decided). That is long enough to isolate (about 20 seconds) and not enough to finish a smother or a drain (5 minutes), so beating a fire in its group means overriding the countdown. The captain can also eject sooner by hand at any time.
- **Override.** A guarded OVERRIDE switch on the panel (lift the cover, then press) holds that group's countdown, and pressing it again resumes the countdown where it stood, so an override can be undone. While it is held the fire runs the spread clock (about 6 minutes to the next group) instead, and the panel shows both the intensity and the spread time.
- **Eject now.** A separate guarded EJECT NOW switch jettisons at once, from any state.
- **Fire going out.** Smothering and draining lower the intensity bar. If it reaches zero the fire is out and the countdown stops by itself.
- **Spread and breach.** If the fire spreads, the neighbouring group's countdown starts at 30 seconds. A group whose pressure cell has breached cannot be overridden: the auto-eject stands.
- **Engineering annulus.** Its countdown begins with an automatic reactor scram at 30 seconds, because a live shield is never ejected.
- **Who presses.** Auto and Supervised stations run rungs 1 to 4, but OVERRIDE and EJECT NOW are always the captain's.

**Ejecting a sink group.** Each of the seven tank groups is built as a cassette on pyrotechnic bolts, so a burning one can be thrown clear.

- **Which groups.** The four annulus halves, the two T-aft flank tanks and the keel reserve go outboard at about 2 m/s. The engineering annulus is part of the reactor shield: ejecting it needs a scram first and leaves bay D open to radiation, so it is the worst choice and the last.
- **What it costs.** The group's share of the sink goes with it: about 16 percent for a 250 m³ group, 12.5 percent for a 200 m³ one, and about 125 t of isotopically pure lithium-7. The loss stays until a yard fits a new cassette, which is a dock cost and the dearest part of any refit. Ejecting stops the fire; it does not cool the rest of the sink.
- **What it becomes.** A bright space object: a hot lithium shell at 900 K or more, with a crimson flare for the first minutes (burning lithium is crimson) and then a dull red glow of about 8 MW that fades over many hours. In the engine it is a heat-only contact that decays slowly.
- **What it is good for.** A decoy, since against a ship with its fins in, 8 MW is a loud signal. A marker, because it tells a hunter where the ship was and that it was in trouble. And salvage: 125 t of lithium-7 floating in the dark is a later job for anyone who finds it.
- **Interface.** Each group shows NORMAL, HOT, FIRE, ISOLATED, DRAINED or EJECTED on the annunciator, and the damage-control panel carries Isolate, Drain and Smother, the countdown with its guarded OVERRIDE, and a guarded EJECT NOW.

## Crew spaces, dorsal airlock and movement

The ship was built for about 75 and is flown by five or six, so most crew space is cold, made-up or empty.

**Berths.** The CO has the stateroom, a 3.4 m round pod under the CIC's floor hatch at the head of deck A2, with the only door on the ship that closes. Six officers' racks sit in three wedge cabins on deck A2, one of the two sides in each kept for visitors. Ratings sleep in the 24 racks wrapped around the tube bases in the missile bay. Racks are shared by watch: only one section sleeps at a time, so 24 racks serve about 60 ratings. A rack is 0.75 m by 2.0 m with 0.8 m of headroom, and most are folded flat with crates strapped in.

**Mess and galley.** Deck B1 is a round room, 9.6 m across, with a ring table for 28: one off-watch section at a time. It is the first deck aft of the bow bays, so everyone walking the ship passes through it. Fenwick's galley is a crescent along one wall. Utility (heads, showers, laundry, the unused sickbay) is on B3, two decks aft of the mess.

**Dorsal airlock.** The airlock is a side chamber on the dorsal wall of the junction (B2), reaching up through the annulus to the collar.

- **Collar:** a 2.2 m docking collar on the dorsal fairing at x about 31, with an iris outer hatch. A telescoping soft-dock tube of about 6 m reaches another ship's collar, which is how boarding works.
- **Chamber:** 2.8 m round and 4.2 m long, lying radially, with six suit lockers, a bench and a drain. It passes through the 2 m ring in a sealed tube, so the water tanks round it never touch the lock.
- **Inner hatch:** 1.2 m round, in the dorsal wall of the junction.
- **Second lock:** a 1.4 m ventral service hatch in the sink machinery hall for lithium fill and yard work.

**Moving about.** Two 1.4 m round stair shafts, one port and one starboard, run the full height of bays A, B, C and D, each with a ladder and a 1.2 m round side door into every deck. It is the Rocinante's skyscraper model, standing on its tail with the bow at the top: going up a deck means climbing toward the bow. The shafts are built into the cell wall, half in the room and half in the annulus, because in C and D the thermal tower and the 8.4 m reactor shield well take the middle of the room (the shafts clear the shield by about 0.1 m). Two shafts are also two routes: if one is on fire, flooded or blocked, the other still works. In the game the player clicks the ladder and a short animation plays (grab a rung, swing through the door), not free climbing. From the control room to the reactor is one straight line of about 80 m and about fifteen hatches; the missile hold is the long middle, 31 m that the gantry car runs on the axis (the shafts continue there as caged ladders with a landing about every 6 m). A 1.2 m crawl along the keel line, in the annulus outside the cells, is a second route for damage control. Under thrust above about 1 g nobody walks. The CIC has its tilted couches for the three officer stations and the CO, and every other bay has wall-folded brace couches.

**Decisions, 15 November 2026.**

- 140 m hull with round rooms: accepted.
- Missile count: 10 live tubes (60), 2 probe tubes, 12 salvage tubes: accepted.
- Gravity is Newtonian only: no gravity generator, no spun sections (spinning adds heat), magnetised floors.
- Main fusion pods accepted; reaction mass is water, and the fusion fuel is carried as deuterium and helium-3 cassettes (decided 15 November).
- Lithium fires are the consequence of overloading the sink. Venting to vacuum does not extinguish them, because thermal runaway and hot lithium need no free oxygen.
- The salvage tubes are the strongroom for smuggled AI-touched salvage, which does not count toward the integration meter and does not appear in the Tally.
- Pod heat goes into the lithium sinks. The vent fins reject it continuously in normal running; retracted for silent running, the sink only warms.
- Ejection stays. A fire in a tank group starts a 30-second countdown to automatic ejection on the damage-control panel. The captain can override it from the panel, undo the override, or eject sooner by hand. A core overload is a possible later mechanic and is left out for now.
- Ring rooms: accepted. Each pressure cell is a drum along the ship axis and each room is a round disc (a puck) about 10 m across and 3 m thick, stacked fore and aft, about three to a bay; the missile bay is the one long hall. Under thrust "down" is the aft face, always, because the engines fire aft and the ship flips to brake. Ships accelerate, flip, and brake hard; the flight model and the click-a-waypoint planner are in docs/maneuver_model.md.
- Deck layout follows the Rocinante's "skyscraper" model: a stack of decks joined by two stair shafts (one each side, built into the wall) with a ladder and a round door into each floor, laid along the ship's axis with the bow at the top. Moving between decks is a click on the ladder that plays a short animation (grab a rung, swing through the door) rather than free climbing. Decision (15 November): two wall-built shafts replace the single central trunk, which leaves the middle of every deck free and gives a second route.
- All hulls are black, ours and every other ship's. Colour is used only as cue marks: maker bands, tube-load rings and hazard stripes (see Finish and colour cues).
- Drive thrust: keep 0.15 g at 100 percent, stated in the fiction as a lost-technology yard pod. The speed rungs stay as they are.
- Propellant: water as reaction mass, held in forward tanks (250 t of drive water, a 20 t crew reserve and a 60 m³ settling tank), plus fuel cassettes (deuterium and helium-3, a magazine of 8) as the fusion fuel. Both are common enough to find or buy and scarce enough to force salvage. Water sets how far the ship goes; cassettes set how long its pods run and whether it can jump.
- Tube geometry (16 November): tube caps sit flush on the dorsal skin, so each 8.6 m tube has 2.0 m in the ring and 6.6 m in the cell, ending 1.6 m past the axis; the 3 m ventral ordnance gallery fits in the 3.4 m below the bases. Tube pitch is 2.45 m (29.4 m for twelve).
- Complement: about 75 designed, five or six working.
